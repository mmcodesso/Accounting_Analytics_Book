## Purpose and learning outcomes

Tutorial 12.1 found two approved payroll registers of an Account Executive who had left, with no payment record, and Tutorial 12.2 found the same employee named as approver of purchase orders dated after leaving. The chief audit executive asks for a test of the pay of everyone who had left and of the approval of every register and payment, added to the query library. The audit mindset: classify each result as an isolated exception or a pattern, because that decides what is reported, and let the cause, not the count, decide it. A few registers can still recur and share a cause; one approver for every register, including the approver's own pay, points to a design weakness, to confirm against the policy and any independent review. A population test that returns a handful of rows is still a finding. The analytics type is diagnostic, and the recommendations are prescriptive.

Students can:

- test pay after termination and compare it with the employee's usual pay (Requirement 1);
- test the approval of registers and payments by approver, by timing and for self-approval (Requirements 2 and 3);
- classify results as isolated or systematic and say what each shows about the control's design (Requirement 4);
- write findings with a recommendation for each (Requirement 5).

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution Core S2 M5 (risk of fraud and noncompliance) and ISC M2; IMA Internal Control; IFAC IES 2 (f) and (g); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

## Step-by-step solution

### Requirement (1): Pay after termination

**Step 1. List the registers paid after the termination date, with the usual pay.** The correlated subquery in the SELECT list averages the employee's other registers paid on or before the termination date; a second one counts the payments.

```sql
-- Exercise 12.5, test R1: registers paid after the employee's termination date
-- Population: all payroll registers; expected: final paychecks only
SELECT pr.PayrollRegisterID, pr.EmployeeID, e.JobTitle,
    ec.CostCenterName AS EmployeeCenter,
    rc.CostCenterName AS RegisterCenter, e.TerminationDate,
    pp.PeriodStartDate, pp.PeriodEndDate, pp.PayDate, pr.GrossPay,
    (SELECT ROUND(AVG(p2.GrossPay), 2)
     FROM PayrollRegister AS p2
        INNER JOIN PayrollPeriod AS q2
            ON q2.PayrollPeriodID = p2.PayrollPeriodID
     WHERE p2.EmployeeID = pr.EmployeeID
        AND q2.PayDate <= e.TerminationDate
        AND p2.GrossPay > 0) AS UsualGrossPay,
    (SELECT COUNT(*)
     FROM PayrollPayment AS pay
     WHERE pay.PayrollRegisterID = pr.PayrollRegisterID) AS Payments
FROM PayrollRegister AS pr
    INNER JOIN PayrollPeriod AS pp
        ON pp.PayrollPeriodID = pr.PayrollPeriodID
    INNER JOIN Employee AS e ON e.EmployeeID = pr.EmployeeID
    INNER JOIN CostCenter AS ec ON ec.CostCenterID = e.CostCenterID
    INNER JOIN CostCenter AS rc ON rc.CostCenterID = pr.CostCenterID
WHERE e.TerminationDate IS NOT NULL
    AND pp.PayDate > e.TerminationDate
ORDER BY pp.PayDate;
```

![A mock of the registers paid after the termination date, the list the chief audit executive opens first.](../figures/chapter-12-sql-audit-analytics/IF-12-5-A.pdf)

**Check:** 3 rows, employee 81, Account Executive of the Sales cost center, terminated 2024-04-26: register 840 (period from 2024-04-22, paid 2024-05-10); register 2646 (period from 2024-12-16, paid 2025-01-03); register 5420 (period from 2025-12-15, paid 2026-01-02). Each pays $9,424.91 gross, about four times the usual $2,217.25, and each is charged to the Executive cost center. Only register 840, whose period holds the termination date, could be a final paycheck, and even it pays about four times the usual amount. **Watch for:** three wrong versions.

- Comparing the period start with the termination date returns 2 rows and misses register 840, the one whose period holds the date. The period end finds the same three here.
- Averaging every register of the employee, the three included, gives $4,182.98 as the usual pay and hides the multiple.
- Test R2 shows that the chief executive has 74 registers in 77 pay periods and 0 in these three: the chief executive's pay for them appears to be recorded under employee 81. Registers 2646 and 5420 have no payment record (Tutorial 12.1).

### Requirement (2): Who approved the registers

**Step 2. Count by approver.** Group by the approver's key and count the registers approved on their pay date.

```sql
-- Exercise 12.5, test R3: registers approved by each approver, and on their pay date
-- Population: all payroll registers; expected: approval shared, before the pay date
SELECT pr.ApprovedByEmployeeID, e.JobTitle, COUNT(*) AS Registers,
    SUM(CASE WHEN pr.ApprovedDate = pp.PayDate THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
        AS ApprovedOnPayDate
FROM PayrollRegister AS pr
    INNER JOIN PayrollPeriod AS pp
        ON pp.PayrollPeriodID = pr.PayrollPeriodID
    INNER JOIN Employee AS e ON e.EmployeeID = pr.ApprovedByEmployeeID
GROUP BY pr.ApprovedByEmployeeID, e.JobTitle
ORDER BY Registers DESC;
```

**Check:** one row: employee 5, the Accounting Manager, 8,197 registers, 8,197 approved on the pay date. **Watch for:** counting the approvals through an inner join to PayrollPayment returns 8,194: the three registers with no payment drop out, the three the audit most needs.

### Requirement (3): Self-approval and payment before approval

**Step 3. Find the registers approved by the employee they pay.**

```sql
-- Exercise 12.5, test R4: registers approved by the employee they pay
-- Population: all payroll registers; expected: no rows
SELECT pr.EmployeeID, e.JobTitle, COUNT(*) AS Registers,
    ROUND(SUM(pr.NetPay), 2) AS NetPay
FROM PayrollRegister AS pr
    INNER JOIN Employee AS e ON e.EmployeeID = pr.EmployeeID
WHERE pr.ApprovedByEmployeeID = pr.EmployeeID
GROUP BY pr.EmployeeID, e.JobTitle;
```

**Check:** one row: 77 registers, $282,646.21 net, the approver's own pay.

**Step 4. Find the payments made before their register was approved.**

```sql
-- Exercise 12.5, test R5: payments made before their register was approved
-- Population: all payroll payments; expected: no rows
SELECT pay.PayrollPaymentID, pay.PayrollRegisterID, e.JobTitle,
    pay.PaymentDate, pr.ApprovedDate, pr.NetPay
FROM PayrollPayment AS pay
    INNER JOIN PayrollRegister AS pr
        ON pr.PayrollRegisterID = pay.PayrollRegisterID
    INNER JOIN Employee AS e ON e.EmployeeID = pr.EmployeeID
WHERE pay.PaymentDate < pr.ApprovedDate
ORDER BY pay.PayrollPaymentID;
```

**Check:** 3 rows, payments 2, 2647, 5421, $23,099.19 net in all, each to the chief financial officer the day before approval. **Watch for:** `<=` in place of `<` flags 8,194 payments, because nearly every payment is made on the approval day, and the three are buried among them.

### Requirement (4): Isolated or systematic

**Step 5. Classify.** The post-termination registers are three registers of one employee, in three pay years, all charged to the Executive cost center, and two of them have no payment record. They are few, but they recur, so they may share a cause in how pay is mapped to employees: investigate the common cause and trace them to the bank, and accept either label if it is reasoned. The approval results are a pattern: one person approved every register, each on its pay date, the 77 that pay the approver among them, and three payments went out a day before approval. The pattern points to a weakness in the design of the control rather than a lapse, unless an independent review that the records do not show makes up for it.

### Requirement (5): Findings and recommendations

**Step 6. Write the list.** Pay after termination: trace registers 840, 2646 and 5420 to the bank, confirm with the chief executive, correct the master data; deactivate pay at termination and review the registers of former employees each period. Missing payments: reconcile registers to payments and to the bank every pay period. One approver: a second approver for registers and an independent approver for the approver's own pay. Payments before approval: release payments only after approval, enforced by the system. Add tests R1 to R5 to the library.

## What a strong answer says

- **(1)** Three registers, one employee, the same gross each time, charged to the Executive center, with no register for the chief executive in those periods. The answer states what the records show, not who received the money, and asks for the bank records.
- **(2) and (3)** One approver for every register, including the approver's own and each on the pay date, points to a design weakness. A strong answer asks what a second reviewer would have to see, and whether any review outside these records exists.
- **(4)** Isolated versus pattern, with the reason. A strong answer can call the post-termination registers either, if it weighs their recurrence and asks for the common cause. A weak answer calls all of it a control failure, or none of it, or calls the registers fraud.
- **(5)** Each finding has a recommendation that changes the design. Adding the tests to the library is a plus.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **The period start for the pay date.** *Symptom:* 2 registers. *Response:* ask which register's period holds the termination date.
- **An average that includes the exception.** *Symptom:* a usual pay of $4,182.98. *Response:* ask which registers the average should rest on.
- **An inner join to payments.** *Symptom:* 8,194 registers. *Response:* ask which registers have no payment.
- **`<=` on the dates.** *Symptom:* 8,194 payments. *Response:* ask what "before" means.
- **Naming the recipient.** *Symptom:* "the chief executive was paid twice". *Response:* ask what the records show and what only the bank shows.
