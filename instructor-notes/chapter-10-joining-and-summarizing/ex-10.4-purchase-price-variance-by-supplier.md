## Purpose and learning outcomes

Exercise 9.2 showed that account 5060 records the difference between supplier invoices and the order prices at which goods were received, plus purchase tax that cannot be recovered. The purchasing manager asks which suppliers bill above the order price and whether the account grew because prices rose or because Charles River bought more. The exercise ties a ledger total to the documents behind it with joins, and then asks a bridge question that the data can only partly answer. It is the chapter's longest query work. The analytics type is diagnostic.

By the end, students can:

- total a ledger account by supplier through the source document (outcomes 1 and 3);
- recompute the amount from the invoice and order lines and explain the cents of difference (outcomes 1, 5 and 6);
- count lines above, below and at a price with three conditions that add up to the population (outcome 3);
- express a variance as a rate of the amount invoiced, and say what the comparison cannot show.

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution BAR M5 (cost accounting and variance analysis) and BAR M10 (data models and joins); IMA Reporting and Control (cost accounting) and Strategy, Planning, and Performance (strategic cost management); IFAC IES 2 (b) and (i); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

## Before you assign

- **Files and software.** CharlesRiver_Work.sqlite and a script `Ex 10.4.sql`. Students need Tutorials 10.1 and 10.2 and Exercise 9.2.
- **Data conditions.** The ledger rows of 5060 come from PurchaseInvoice documents, and their descriptions record a favorable variance, an unfavorable variance or nonrecoverable purchase tax. The year's closing entry also posts to 5060, as a JournalEntry, so the join to PurchaseInvoice drops it. Some invoice lines carry no POLineID, and the inner join leaves them out.
- **Verify first.** The variance postings of the year, `Description LIKE '%purchase variance'` on the account, number 19,659 and net $109,566.58.

## Step-by-step solution

### Requirement (1): The ledger by supplier

**Step 1. Total the variance postings by supplier and keep the five largest.**

```sql
-- Requirement (1): the five suppliers with the largest variance in fiscal 2026
-- Population: GLEntry, 5060, fiscal 2026, purchase variance postings; expected: Bell, Pruitt and David first, 6,158.65
SELECT s.SupplierID, s.SupplierName, COUNT(*) AS Postings,
    ROUND(SUM(gl.Debit) - SUM(gl.Credit), 2) AS PriceVariance
FROM GLEntry AS gl
    INNER JOIN Account AS a ON a.AccountID = gl.AccountID
    INNER JOIN PurchaseInvoice AS pi
        ON gl.SourceDocumentType = 'PurchaseInvoice'
        AND pi.PurchaseInvoiceID = gl.SourceDocumentID
    INNER JOIN Supplier AS s ON s.SupplierID = pi.SupplierID
WHERE a.AccountNumber = 5060 AND gl.FiscalYear = 2026
    AND gl.Description LIKE '%purchase variance'
GROUP BY s.SupplierID, s.SupplierName
ORDER BY PriceVariance DESC
LIMIT 5;
```

**Step 2. Total the ledger's variance.**

```sql
-- Requirement (1): the ledger total of the variance postings of fiscal 2026
-- Population: as above, every supplier; expected: 19,659 postings, 109,566.58
SELECT COUNT(*) AS Postings,
    ROUND(SUM(gl.Debit) - SUM(gl.Credit), 2) AS PriceVariance
FROM GLEntry AS gl
    INNER JOIN Account AS a ON a.AccountID = gl.AccountID
WHERE a.AccountNumber = 5060 AND gl.FiscalYear = 2026
    AND gl.Description LIKE '%purchase variance';
```

**Check:** the five largest are 65 Bell, Pruitt and David $6,158.65; 56 Velasquez-Baxter $5,170.56; 100 Collins-Johnson $4,931.16; 17 Brooks, Johnson and Olson $4,719.17; 16 Berger, Stanley and Cooper $4,410.07. The total is $109,566.58 over 19,659 postings. The five hold 23.2% of it among 103 suppliers, so billing above the order price is widespread. **Watch for:** without the description condition Step 1 totals $176,011.36, because the tax rows are added ($66,444.78; the join drops the closing entry), and the five largest become suppliers 65, 16, 100, 33, 56. Step 2 without it reads $0.00, because the closing entry cancels the account. With only the unfavorable description the total is $167,063.83, a gross amount, and the five become 65, 33, 17, 16, 100. `LIKE '%favorable%'` matches both kinds of posting, 19,659 rows, the same as `'%purchase variance'`.

### Requirement (2): The variance from the documents

**Step 3. Recompute it from the invoice and order lines.**

```sql
-- Requirement (2): the variance recalculated from invoices dated in 2026
-- Population: invoice lines with a purchase-order line; expected: 19,866 lines, 109,566.20
SELECT COUNT(*) AS InvoiceLines,
    ROUND(SUM(pil.Quantity * (pil.UnitCost - pol.UnitCost)), 2)
        AS PriceVariance
FROM PurchaseInvoiceLine AS pil
    INNER JOIN PurchaseInvoice AS pi
        ON pi.PurchaseInvoiceID = pil.PurchaseInvoiceID
    INNER JOIN PurchaseOrderLine AS pol
        ON pol.POLineID = pil.POLineID
WHERE pi.InvoiceDate BETWEEN '2026-01-01' AND '2026-12-31';
```

**Step 4. Compare the two by supplier.** A hint, beyond the exercise, which asks only for the totals. The model script joins two derived tables on SupplierID, one for the ledger and one for the lines, and reports the number of suppliers, both totals and the largest difference in one row.

**Check:** 19,866 lines and $109,566.20, $0.38 below the ledger. The ledger posts each line's stored LineTotal less the GRNI it clears, both rounded, which add $1.99 and $1.61 to the unit-cost amounts, and a query of the model script reproduces the ledger from them. By supplier the two agree to within $0.22. **Watch for:** subtracting the two LineTotal columns returns -$16,021,206.87, because an order line's total covers its whole quantity while an invoice line bills part of it; the difference of prices times the invoiced quantity is the variance. The inner join leaves out 125 of the year's 19,991 invoice lines, the ones with no order line.

### Requirement (3): Above, below and at the order price

**Step 5. Count the lines billed above the order price.**

```sql
-- Requirement (3): fiscal 2026 invoice lines billed above the order price
-- Population: the 19,866 lines of (2); expected: 12,413
SELECT COUNT(*) AS InvoiceLines
FROM PurchaseInvoiceLine AS pil
    INNER JOIN PurchaseInvoice AS pi
        ON pi.PurchaseInvoiceID = pil.PurchaseInvoiceID
    INNER JOIN PurchaseOrderLine AS pol
        ON pol.POLineID = pil.POLineID
WHERE pi.InvoiceDate BETWEEN '2026-01-01' AND '2026-12-31'
    AND pil.UnitCost > pol.UnitCost;
```

The other two queries of the script differ only in the last condition: `pil.UnitCost < pol.UnitCost` for below and `pil.UnitCost = pol.UnitCost` for at the price. The script also holds a query that finds the range of the invoice prices against the order prices.

**Check:** above 12,413, below 7,286, at the price 167, which add up to the 19,866 lines; the invoice prices run from 1.5% below to 2.6% above the order prices. **Watch for:** `<>` returns 19,699 rows, the lines above and below together. A half-cent tolerance finds the same 167 lines at the price, so equality on these REAL values is safe here.

### Requirement (4): By year

**Step 6. Total the amount invoiced and the variance for each year.**

```sql
-- Requirement (4): amount invoiced against orders and variance by year, 2024 to 2026
-- Population: invoice lines with a purchase-order line, dated 2024 to 2026; expected: 2024 0.48%, 2025 0.47%, 2026 0.51%
SELECT SUBSTR(pi.InvoiceDate, 1, 4) AS InvoiceYear,
    COUNT(*) AS InvoiceLines,
    ROUND(SUM(pil.Quantity * pil.UnitCost), 2) AS AmountInvoiced,
    ROUND(SUM(pil.LineTotal), 2) AS LineTotals,
    ROUND(SUM(pil.Quantity * (pil.UnitCost - pol.UnitCost)), 2)
        AS PriceVariance,
    ROUND(100.0 * SUM(pil.Quantity * (pil.UnitCost - pol.UnitCost))
        / SUM(pil.Quantity * pil.UnitCost), 2) AS VariancePct
FROM PurchaseInvoiceLine AS pil
    INNER JOIN PurchaseInvoice AS pi
        ON pi.PurchaseInvoiceID = pil.PurchaseInvoiceID
    INNER JOIN PurchaseOrderLine AS pol
        ON pol.POLineID = pil.POLineID
WHERE pi.InvoiceDate BETWEEN '2024-01-01' AND '2026-12-31'
GROUP BY InvoiceYear
ORDER BY InvoiceYear;
```

![A mock of the variance and the amount invoiced by year, for the discussion of price against volume.](../figures/chapter-10-joining-and-summarizing/IF-10-4-A.pdf)

**Check:** 2024: $13,491,998.13 invoiced, variance $64,631.92, 0.48%; 2025: $17,468,202.42 invoiced, variance $82,377.43, 0.47%; 2026: $21,612,260.34 invoiced, variance $109,566.20, 0.51%. The rate stays near half a percent while both amounts grow. Of the variance's growth from 2024 to 2026, $44,934.28, $38,899.21 (87%) comes with the larger amount invoiced at the 2024 rate and $6,035.07 from the higher 2026 rate. **Watch for:** dividing by the amount of every invoice line, including those with no order line, gives 0.44%, 0.44%, 0.48% against the matched 0.48%, 0.47%, 0.51%. The LineTotals column differs from the amount by $0.80 to $1.99 a year, the rounding of each line.

## What a strong answer says

- **Which suppliers bill above the order price.** Many do. The ledger's variance is $109,566.58, and the five largest suppliers hold 23.2% of it among 103. Of the 19,866 lines linked to purchase-order lines, 12,413 are billed above the order price and 7,286 below, within 1.5% below to 2.6% above. The documents give $109,566.20, within $0.38 of the ledger, the rounding of the two amounts the ledger posts.
- **Price or volume.** The variance is a near-steady share of what is invoiced against orders, so the account grew with the amount invoiced. A strong answer says that the amount invoiced mixes quantity, order prices and mix, so the comparison cannot say how much of it is more buying.
- **What the comparison cannot show.** It measures the invoice against the purchase order, so a price rise already built into the order prices does not appear. Testing whether Charles River pays too much needs order prices compared with earlier prices, price lists or the standard cost.

A weak memo says that suppliers raised their prices, or that the company bought more, from one of the two facts alone.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **The description condition dropped.** *Symptom:* a total of $176,011.36 by supplier, or $0.00 for the ledger. *Response:* ask what else posts to the account.
- **Only the unfavorable postings.** *Symptom:* a total of $167,063.83. *Response:* ask what the favorable postings do to the net.
- **LineTotals subtracted.** *Symptom:* -$16,021,206.87. *Response:* ask what an order line's total covers.
- **The cents called an error.** *Symptom:* a note that the totals disagree by $0.38, or each line's difference rounded ($109,566.65). *Response:* ask which two amounts the ledger posts for each line.
- **The wrong denominator.** *Symptom:* rates of 0.44%, 0.44%, 0.48%. *Response:* ask which invoice lines have an order price to compare with.
- **Volume asserted.** *Symptom:* a memo that says Charles River simply bought more. *Response:* ask what else the amount invoiced contains.
