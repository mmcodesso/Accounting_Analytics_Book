## Purpose and learning outcomes

The sales manager wants to know which customers place the largest orders and whether order size differs by segment. The exercise is the chapter's first plain GROUP BY: a join, three aggregates, HAVING and a sort. Its lesson is the Watch out of the chapter: group by the key, not the name. The analytics type is descriptive, with a diagnostic habit: compare the row counts of two groupings and explain the difference.

By the end, students can:

- join SalesOrder to Customer and summarize by customer with COUNT, SUM and AVG (outcomes 1 and 3);
- show that a grouping by name merges two customers, by comparing row counts (outcome 3);
- filter groups with HAVING and rank them with ORDER BY and LIMIT (outcome 3);
- say what an average by segment tells a sales manager.

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution BAR M10 (data models and joins) and Core S1 M8 (data mining and analysis of financial data); IMA Data Analytics and Insights (data analysis, data tools and techniques); IFAC IES 2 (b) and (i); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

## Before you assign

- **Files and software.** CharlesRiver_Work.sqlite and a script `Ex 10.3.sql`. Students need Tutorial 10.1 (joins, GROUP BY, HAVING).

## Step-by-step solution

### Requirement (1): Orders and average value by customer

**Step 1. Group the orders by customer key.**

```sql
-- Requirement (1): orders and average order value by customer
-- Population: SalesOrder, every order; expected: 211 customers, Glenn-Estrada first
SELECT c.CustomerID, c.CustomerName, c.CustomerSegment,
    COUNT(*) AS Orders,
    ROUND(SUM(so.OrderTotal), 2) AS TotalOrdered,
    ROUND(AVG(so.OrderTotal), 2) AS AverageOrder
FROM SalesOrder AS so
    INNER JOIN Customer AS c ON c.CustomerID = so.CustomerID
GROUP BY c.CustomerID, c.CustomerName, c.CustomerSegment
ORDER BY AverageOrder DESC;
```

**Step 2. Check the population.** Two short queries, the model answer's own, confirm the totals.

```sql
-- Requirement (1): all orders, the customers who placed them, and the average
-- Population: SalesOrder; expected: 5,819 orders, 211 customers, average 15,036.96
SELECT COUNT(*) AS Orders,
    COUNT(DISTINCT CustomerID) AS Customers,
    ROUND(AVG(OrderTotal), 2) AS AverageOrder
FROM SalesOrder;
```

```sql
-- Requirement (1): the customers in the master file
-- Population: Customer; expected: 220, so 9 have no orders
SELECT COUNT(*) AS Customers
FROM Customer;
```

**Check:** 211 rows, led by Glenn-Estrada (54 orders, $26,202.95), Miller-Hernandez (50 orders, $25,394.03), Eaton, Robertson and Grant (44 orders, $25,350.26). The population is 5,819 orders from 211 of 220 customers, so 9 have none, with an average of $15,036.96. **Watch for:** a LEFT JOIN from Customer with `COUNT(*)` returns 220 rows, and the 9 customers with no orders show one order and a NULL average. The average of the customers' averages is $12,804.21, not the order average. Joined to the order lines, `SUM(so.OrderTotal)` reads $394,810,333.58 against $87,500,091.56.

### Requirement (2): The same analysis by name

**Step 3. Group by CustomerName.**

```sql
-- Requirement (2): the same analysis grouped by CustomerName
-- Population: as (1); expected: 210 rows, one fewer than (1)
SELECT c.CustomerName, COUNT(*) AS Orders,
    ROUND(SUM(so.OrderTotal), 2) AS TotalOrdered,
    ROUND(AVG(so.OrderTotal), 2) AS AverageOrder
FROM SalesOrder AS so
    INNER JOIN Customer AS c ON c.CustomerID = so.CustomerID
GROUP BY c.CustomerName
ORDER BY AverageOrder DESC;
```

**Step 4. Find the names that cover more than one customer.**

```sql
-- Requirement (2): names that cover more than one customer with orders
-- Population: as (1); expected: Brown LLC, 2 customers
SELECT c.CustomerName,
    COUNT(DISTINCT c.CustomerID) AS Customers,
    COUNT(*) AS Orders
FROM SalesOrder AS so
    INNER JOIN Customer AS c ON c.CustomerID = so.CustomerID
GROUP BY c.CustomerName
HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT c.CustomerID) > 1;
```

**Check:** 210 rows, one fewer than Step 1. Brown LLC has 2 customers: customer 5 (Design Trade, 20 orders, $10,258.01), customer 173 (Strategic, 51 orders, $24,276.43). By name they merge into one row of 71 orders averaging $20,327.58, which describes neither; the merged row is number 21 in the ranking by name, while the two customers are numbers 8 and 137 in the ranking by key. **Watch for:** adding CustomerSegment to the grouping hides Brown LLC, because its customers sit in different segments, and leaves only Smith Ltd. The model script also lists every shared name with its orders, a left join that shows that only customer 170 of Smith Ltd has orders.

### Requirement (3): Customers with at least 20 orders

**Step 5. Keep the groups with HAVING and take the ten highest averages.**

```sql
-- Requirement (3): the ten highest averages among customers with at least 20 orders
-- Population: as (1), HAVING 20 orders or more; expected: Glenn-Estrada first
SELECT c.CustomerID, c.CustomerName, c.CustomerSegment,
    COUNT(*) AS Orders,
    ROUND(AVG(so.OrderTotal), 2) AS AverageOrder
FROM SalesOrder AS so
    INNER JOIN Customer AS c ON c.CustomerID = so.CustomerID
GROUP BY c.CustomerID, c.CustomerName, c.CustomerSegment
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 20
ORDER BY AverageOrder DESC
LIMIT 10;
```

**Check:** 10 rows, led by Glenn-Estrada; 138 of the 211 customers have at least 20 orders. The ten are the same ten as without the HAVING, because every one of the ten has at least that many orders; the condition protects against a small sample and changes nothing here. **Watch for:** `WHERE COUNT(*) >= 20` stops with misuse of aggregate; a sort without DESC returns the ten lowest averages.

### Requirement (4): By segment

**Step 6. Group by CustomerSegment.**

```sql
-- Requirement (4): orders and average order value by segment
-- Population: as (1); expected: 5 segments, Strategic highest
SELECT c.CustomerSegment, COUNT(*) AS Orders,
    COUNT(DISTINCT c.CustomerID) AS Customers,
    ROUND(AVG(so.OrderTotal), 2) AS AverageOrder
FROM SalesOrder AS so
    INNER JOIN Customer AS c ON c.CustomerID = so.CustomerID
GROUP BY c.CustomerSegment
ORDER BY AverageOrder DESC;
```

**Check:** 5 rows, Strategic 1,136 orders at $23,386.13; Wholesale 2,771 orders at $15,993.96; Design Services 239 orders at $14,742.20; Design Trade 956 orders at $10,062.99; Small Business 717 orders at $4,840.43. The largest segment's average is 4.8 times the smallest's.

### Requirement (5): The note

**Step 7. Outline the note.** See "What a strong answer says".

## What a strong answer says

- **Requirement (5), the note.** Order size differs sharply by segment: the segment averages run from $4,840.43 to $23,386.13, a ratio of 4.8, so the mix of segments matters as much as the number of orders. The customers with the largest orders are Glenn-Estrada, Miller-Hernandez and Eaton, Robertson and Grant.
- **Why the key.** The key identifies a customer and the name does not. Grouping by name returns 210 rows instead of 211, because two customers share Brown LLC, and the merged row describes neither.

A weak note quotes the segment averages without a comparison, or answers the question about grouping with "the names are the same" and no row counts.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **Grouping by name.** *Symptom:* 210 rows. *Response:* ask which customer the row belongs to.
- **A left join and `COUNT(*)`.** *Symptom:* 9 customers with one order and a NULL average. *Response:* ask what the average of nothing is.
- **OrderTotal summed over the order lines.** *Symptom:* a total of $394,810,333.58. *Response:* ask at what level OrderTotal is recorded.
- **An average of averages.** *Symptom:* an overall average of $12,804.21. *Response:* ask what weight each customer's average should carry.
- **COUNT in WHERE.** *Symptom:* misuse of aggregate. *Response:* ask whether the condition is about a row or a group.
- **A grouping that hides the duplicate.** *Symptom:* only Smith Ltd found. *Response:* ask which columns decide whether two customers are the same.
