## Purpose and learning outcomes

The audit committee asks whether the controls behind the tests are getting better or worse. Counts alone cannot answer that when the number of documents changes from year to year, and the exceptions already explained, the year-end closes and the recurring payroll test, hide what changes in the rest. The exercise builds the rate that sets those two aside, and its technical lesson is KEEPFILTERS. A rate shows how often the tests find exceptions, not by itself whether a control improved. The analytics type is descriptive, the reading of the trend is diagnostic, and the note's recommendation is prescriptive.

Students can:

- show exceptions, populations and rates by process and year, and say why a total row shows no rate (Requirement 1);
- write a rate that leaves out expected and recurring exceptions and still works when a reader selects a test (Requirement 2);
- chart rates over time (Requirement 3);
- explain why a count and a rate can tell different stories (Requirement 4);
- write a short note on how each process's rate changed and what would show a change in its controls (Requirement 5).

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution ISC M2 (tests of internal controls) and Core S1 M8 (visualization and communicating results); IMA Reporting and Control (internal control) and Strategy, Planning, and Performance (performance management); IFAC IES 2 (g) (governance, risk management and internal control) and (i) (using ICT to analyze data); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

## Step-by-step solution

### Requirement (1): Rates by process and year

**Step 1. Build the matrix.**

a. Add a page named `Ex 16.4`.
b. Add a **Matrix** with Process from Tests on **Rows**, Year on **Columns**, and Exceptions, Population and Rate per 1,000 as **Values**, titled "Exceptions, population, and rates by process and year".
c. Filter Year to the years of the window.

```
Population =
SWITCH (
    SELECTEDVALUE ( Tests[Process] ),
    "Journal entries", COUNTROWS ( JournalEntry ),
    "Purchase orders", COUNTROWS ( PurchaseOrder ),
    "Payroll", COUNTROWS ( PayrollRegister )
)
```

**Check:** each process shows a rate for each year (Expected results), and the grand total row shows none. Population is the number of documents of the process in the filter context, from SELECTEDVALUE on Tests[Process]; across processes it is blank, so a rate that would mix populations is never shown. **Watch for:** a total row that divides all exceptions by all documents reads 4.5 for 2026, a figure no process has.

### Requirement (2): The unexplained rate

**Step 2. Write the measure.**

a. Select **Key Measures** and choose **Modeling > New measure**.
b. Enter the formula below, format it with one decimal, and type `Ex 16.4` in **Display folder** in **Model view**.
c. Add it to the matrix.

```
Unexplained Rate per 1,000 =
DIVIDE (
    CALCULATE (
        [Exceptions],
        KEEPFILTERS ( Exceptions[Disposition] <> "Expected" ),
        KEEPFILTERS ( Tests[TestID] <> "PR SelfApproved" )
    ),
    [Population]
) * 1000
```

**Check:** the journal entries read 52.63, 34.72, 43.77, payroll 0.76, 0.72, 0.72, and the purchase orders equal their rate of all exceptions. Open exceptions and the deficiencies and follow-ups stay in, so it is not the rate of open exceptions, and a label such as "excluding expected closes and recurring self-approval" says what it is. **Watch for:** two errors.

- KEEPFILTERS left out. The matrix still agrees, because Process is a different column from TestID. In a table by test, which a student may add (the model answer's table, titled "Fiscal 2026", is by process), the row JE Weekend reads 77.4 in place of 20.2, and the row PR SelfApproved reads 8.3 where the right answer is blank. The Checks tab holds the blank test.
- An open-exceptions numerator, `ISBLANK ( Exceptions[Disposition] )`, in place of the exclusion of Expected. Payroll reads 0, 0, 0 exceptions a year in place of 2, 2, 2, and the purchase orders 10, 5, 7 in place of 11, 6, 8.

### Requirement (3): The charts

**Step 3. Chart both rates.**

a. Add a **Line chart** with Year on the **X-axis**, Rate per 1,000 on the **Y-axis** and Process on **Legend**, titled "Rate per 1,000 by year".
b. Copy it and replace the measure with the unexplained rate, titled "Unexplained rate per 1,000 by year".

**Check:** two charts of three points per line. The journal entries sit far above the other two processes on a shared axis, so the purchase order line hugs zero: a reason to read the table beside the chart, not the chart alone.

![A mock of the model answer's page, the rates by process and year with the unexplained rate beside them.](../figures/chapter-16-audit-monitoring/IF-16-4-A.pdf)

### Requirement (4): Count and rate

**Step 4. Compare the purchase orders.** In the matrix, read the purchase orders from 2025 to 2026.

**Check:** the exceptions rose by a third (6 to 8) while the orders grew by 28% (6,545 to 8,366), so the rate rose by only 4% (0.92 to 0.96 failed tests per 1,000 orders), nearly flat at the chart's scale and not unchanged. From 2024 the rate fell by two-thirds while orders more than doubled.

### Requirement (5): The note

**Step 5. Judge each process.** The model note says: journal entries are the process to watch. Their unexplained rate fell in 2025 and rose in 2026, mostly through single documents that fail several tests (the opening entry's four in 2024, the debt reclassifications' two each, in 2024 and 2026), while Weekend, Backdated, SelfApproved barely move. Payroll has two exceptions a year once the recurring self-approval test is left out. Its rate of all exceptions is dominated by that test, and the unexplained rate shows the follow-ups and deficiencies that remain. The purchase orders' rate fell by two-thirds from 2024 and rose by only 4% in the last year. A lower rate under the same tests is not by itself better controls, so the note names what would show it, the mix of documents and the causes of the exceptions. The unexplained rate shows what moves once the expected and recurring exceptions are set aside.

## What a strong answer says

- **(1)** The rate is the process's exceptions over its own documents; the total row is blank because the populations differ.
- **(2)** The measure's meaning stated in its label, and the KEEPFILTERS reason: it intersects a reader's selection with the exclusions.
- **(4)** The count rose by a third while orders grew by 28%, so the rate rose much less, by 4.3%: nearly flat at the chart's scale, not unchanged.
- **(5)** A reading of each process's rate, naming the documents behind the journal entries' movement and the design finding behind payroll's flat rate, and what would show whether a control changed.

A strong answer says what the unexplained rate adds: it separates the movement from the two groups already understood. A weak one repeats the rates.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **No KEEPFILTERS.** *Symptom:* JE Weekend at 77.4. *Response:* ask which exceptions the row counts.
- **Open as unexplained.** *Symptom:* payroll at 0 exceptions, because its deficiencies and follow-ups are reviewed. *Response:* ask which dispositions the exercise leaves out.
- **A rate in the total row.** *Symptom:* 4.5. *Response:* ask which population it divides by.
- **Count read as decline.** *Symptom:* "orders got worse". *Response:* ask how many orders there were.
- **Rate read as a control.** *Symptom:* "purchase orders are improving". *Response:* ask what else would have to stay the same.
- **Shared axis.** *Symptom:* a flat purchase order line. *Response:* ask what scale the line needs.
- **No label.** *Symptom:* a measure named "Rate". *Response:* ask what a committee would think it excludes.
