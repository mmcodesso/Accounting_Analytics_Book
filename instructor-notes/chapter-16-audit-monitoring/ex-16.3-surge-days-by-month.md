## Purpose and learning outcomes

Part III traced the growth of the plant's overtime to surge days, days on which every hourly manufacturing employee clocked exactly the same hours, and Chapter 12 found that the Production Manager approved every surge-day entry. The audit committee wants to know each month whether they continue. The exercise is an analytical test, an anomaly test in the chapter's terms, and its technical lesson is a limit of Desktop's Group By. The analytics type is diagnostic. The judgment lesson is in Requirement (5): who should see the result.

Students can:

- group clock entries by day and count the distinct employees and the distinct values of total hours by editing the formula bar (Requirements 2 and 3);
- chart a count and a share by month with the right denominator (Requirement 4);
- describe when a pattern began, how often it recurs and how its share has moved (Requirement 5);
- explain why a test belongs in internal audit's report and not on a manager's page (Requirement 5).

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution Core S1 M8 (data mining and analysis, visualization and communicating results); IMA Data Analytics and Insights (data visualization) and Business Acumen and Operations (operational knowledge); IFAC IES 2 (b) (management accounting) and IES 3 (b) (communicating effectively); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

## Before you assign

- **Files.** The copy after Tutorial 16.3 with its Employee query and Date table. The model answer is page Ex 16.3 of the project, the two queries, and the display folder Ex 16.3.
- **Data conditions.** On every surge day all hourly manufacturing employees clock the same regular and overtime hours (TimeClockEntry), and each entry carries an approval by the Production Manager on the work date. Six employee-days of the plant have two clock entries, none on a surge day, so a count of rows is not a count of employees in general. The test counts the employees who clocked in, as H8 does, and a count against Employee shows they are the whole hourly crew.
- **Verify first.** Employee carries CostCenterID, and the model answer filters ClockEntries to the Manufacturing cost center's ID, 4.

## How to run it

Individual or pairs, about 75 minutes: 15 for Requirement (1), 25 for (2) and (3), 15 for (4), 10 for (5), 10 spare. Do not tell students that Group By cannot count distinct values until they have tried: the failure teaches the limit. Collect the .pbix and the note.

## Step-by-step solution

### Requirement (1): The manufacturing clock entries

**Step 1. Import and filter.**

a. Choose **Home > Get data > Excel workbook**, select T66_TimeClockEntry and choose **Transform Data**. Rename it `ClockEntries`.
b. Keep EmployeeID, WorkDate, RegularHours and OvertimeHours, and set WorkDate to **Date**.
c. Choose **Home > Merge Queries** with **Employee** on EmployeeID and expand CostCenterID.
d. Filter CostCenterID to the Manufacturing cost center.

```
    #"Filtered Rows" = Table.SelectRows(#"Expanded Employee", each ([CostCenterID] = 4)),
    #"Added Custom" = Table.AddColumn(#"Filtered Rows", "TotalHours", each [RegularHours] + [OvertimeHours]),
```

**Check:** ClockEntries holds only manufacturing entries, with a TotalHours column from the next step.

### Requirement (2): One row per day

**Step 2. Group by day.**

a. Add `TotalHours`, regular plus overtime, as in the second step of the block above.
b. Reference ClockEntries as `SurgeDays`. Choose **Transform > Group By**, select **Advanced**, group by WorkDate, and add three aggregations: `Employees` and `HoursValues` with any operation, and `OvertimeHours` as the sum of OvertimeHours.
c. In the formula bar, change the first two aggregations to the distinct counts of EmployeeID and TotalHours.

```
    #"Grouped Rows" = Table.Group(Source, {"WorkDate"}, {{"Employees", each List.Count(List.Distinct([EmployeeID])), Int64.Type}, {"HoursValues", each List.Count(List.Distinct([TotalHours])), Int64.Type}, {"OvertimeHours", each List.Sum([OvertimeHours]), type nullable number}}),
    #"Filtered Rows" = Table.SelectRows(#"Grouped Rows", each [Employees] > 1 and [HoursValues] = 1)
```

**Check:** one row per day with a clock entry, 745 days. **Watch for:** the limit. **Count Distinct Rows** counts whole rows, and the rows of one day differ by employee, so HoursValues never equals 1 on a day with several employees. A student who skips the formula-bar edit, or uses Count Distinct Rows for both columns, finds 0 surge days in the next step: an empty page, with no error.

### Requirement (3): The surge days

**Step 3. Keep the days that qualify and load both queries.**

a. In SurgeDays, filter to Employees greater than 1 and HoursValues equal to 1, the last step of the block above.
b. Choose **Home > Close & Apply**.

**Check:** 93 rows, 12 in 2024, 32 in 2025, 49 in 2026, each with 64 employees. **Watch for:** the cost center filter left out. Other cost centers clock other hours, so a day never has a single value of total hours: 0 surge days. The guard on the number of employees changes nothing today (0 days differ), because every plant day has several entries. It protects a monitor that outlives this data.

### Requirement (4): The page

**Step 4. Relate, measure and chart.**

a. In **Model view**, relate SurgeDays and ClockEntries to the **Date** table on WorkDate.
b. Add the four measures below to the display folder Ex 16.3.
c. On a page named `Ex 16.3`, add a **Clustered column chart** of Date YearMonth and Surge Days, titled "Surge days by month", a **Line and clustered column chart** titled "Surge-day overtime hours by month, and their share of the plant's overtime" (surge overtime as the column, the share as the line), and a **Table** titled "By year". Filter Year to the years of the window.

```
Surge Days = COUNTROWS ( SurgeDays )
Surge Overtime Hours = SUM ( SurgeDays[OvertimeHours] )
Plant Overtime Hours = SUM ( ClockEntries[OvertimeHours] )
Surge Share of Overtime = DIVIDE ( [Surge Overtime Hours], [Plant Overtime Hours] )
```

**Check:** the first surge day is 2024-05-31; from July 2024 surge days occur in 26 of 30 months, about 3 a month, up to 7 in October 2026. The share by year is 35.5%, 58.9%, 70.5%, from 3,456, 9,216, 14,112 surge overtime hours, while overtime on other days is flat (6,273, 6,426, 5,918 hours). **Watch for:** the denominator. Divided by the overtime of SurgeDays alone, the share reads `100%`; it divides by all of the month's overtime in ClockEntries. And the Date table runs to the end of 2027, so without the Year filter the axis runs into empty months.

### Requirement (5): The note

**Step 5. Describe the pattern and place the test.** A strong note gives the first day, the frequency from July 2024, the rising share and the flat overtime elsewhere, then recommends where the test lives: in internal audit's file, reported to the audit committee. The Production Manager approved all 5,952 entries the test flags, and Gonzalez and Hoffman found that telling the people whose transactions are flagged can encourage fraud when detection is weak. The Plant page's overtime share is a management measure, and this is an audit test.

**Instructor check.** In **DAX query view**, run this and compare it with the Expected results.

```dax
EVALUATE ROW ( "Surge days", [Surge Days], "Share 2026", ROUND ( CALCULATE ( [Surge Share of Overtime], 'Date'[Year] = 2026 ), 4 ), "Surge-entry hours (fewest)", CALCULATE ( MIN ( ClockEntries[TotalHours] ), TREATAS ( VALUES ( SurgeDays[WorkDate] ), 'Date'[Date] ) ) )
```

> **Instructor notes, Exercise 16.3 (SQL; Chapter 12 H8):** surge days 12 in 2024, 32 in 2025, 49 in 2026 (93 in all); the first is 2024-05-31, then surge days in 26 of the 30 months from July 2024 (about 3 a month), up to 7 in October 2026; every surge day has all 64 hourly manufacturing employees at 8.0 regular plus 4.5 overtime hours (the test counts the employees who clocked in, as H8 does; a count against Employee shows they are the whole hourly crew). Surge overtime 3,456, 9,216, and 14,112 hours, 0.355, 0.589, and 0.705 of the year's manufacturing overtime; overtime on other days did not grow (6,273, 6,426, 5,918). All 5,952 surge entries were approved by the Production Manager on the work date. Distinct values: Power BI Desktop's Group By has no Count distinct values (Power Query Online only) and its Count Distinct Rows counts whole rows, so the aggregations are edited to List.Count ( List.Distinct ( \[EmployeeID\] ) ) and List.Count ( List.Distinct ( \[TotalHours\] ) ); one value of total hours means everyone clocked the same hours. Six employee-days of the plant have two clock entries, so a count of rows is not a count of employees in general, though on every surge day the two agree. The share's denominator is all of the month's manufacturing overtime, from ClockEntries; filtering ClockEntries itself to surge days would make the share 100%. (5) The production manager approved every entry the test flags; telling the approver what the test detects is the prompt notification that Gonzalez and Hoffman (2018) found can encourage fraud when detection is weak, so the test stays with internal audit and goes to the audit committee. It selects days to corroborate against the rosters, attendance and production records, not proof that the hours are false. The Plant page's overtime share is a management measure; the surge-day test is an audit test.

## What a strong answer says

- **(5)** The pattern with its numbers, as above, and the reason for the test's home: the approver of every flagged entry is the person the report would otherwise reach, so it goes to the people who oversee the control.
- It separates what the test shows (hours identical for the whole crew, approved by one person) from what it does not (why), and asks the production side for the explanation through internal audit.

A strong answer ties the count, the share and the approver together. A weak one lists the months.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **Count Distinct Rows.** *Symptom:* an empty SurgeDays. *Response:* ask what the rows of one day have in common.
- **Formula-bar edit skipped.** *Symptom:* the same empty table. *Response:* ask what HoursValues counts.
- **All cost centers.** *Symptom:* 0 surge days. *Response:* ask who else clocks that day.
- **Wrong denominator.** *Symptom:* a share of `100%`. *Response:* ask what the share is a share of.
- **Axis into the empty future.** *Symptom:* months with no columns after the data end. *Response:* ask where the Date table ends.
- **A count of entries read as employees.** *Symptom:* none today. *Response:* ask what a second entry for one person would do.
