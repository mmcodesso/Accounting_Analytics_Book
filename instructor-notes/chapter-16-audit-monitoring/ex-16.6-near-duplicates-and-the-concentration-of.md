## Purpose and learning outcomes

The chief audit executive asks for two additions: the near-duplicate payments of Chapter 8, a second anomaly test beside Benford's Law, and a measure of how much of each process rests on one approver, which the exceptions by approver do not show. The technical lessons are a table merged with itself and TREATAS. The analytics type is diagnostic, and the second part is a design question: the finding is not a rule that fails but a control with one person in it.

Students can:

- merge a table with itself and keep each pair once (Requirement 1);
- measure the days between two payments and test whether a pair pays one invoice (Requirement 2);
- count the documents an employee approved in tables the employee is not related to (Requirement 3);
- compute shares with the right denominator and compare them with the exceptions by approver (Requirement 4);
- explain why a near-duplicate is an anomaly, and what concentration says about the design of the approvals (Requirement 5).

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution Core S2 M5 (risk of fraud and noncompliance) and ISC M2 (tests of internal controls); IMA Reporting and Control (internal control); IFAC IES 2 (f) (audit) and (g) (governance, risk management and internal control); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

## Step-by-step solution

### Requirement (1): The pairs

**Step 1. Merge the payments with themselves.**

a. Choose **Home > Transform data**, right-click **Payments** and choose **Reference**. Rename the copy `PaymentPairs`.
b. Choose **Home > Merge Queries**, select **Payments** as the second table, click SupplierID and then Amount in both previews, and keep the inner join.
c. Expand DisbursementID, PaymentDate and PurchaseInvoiceID, renamed `PairDisbursementID`, `PairPaymentDate` and `PairInvoiceID`.
d. Keep the rows whose DisbursementID is less than PairDisbursementID and whose dates differ.

```
    #"Merged Queries" = Table.NestedJoin(Source, {"SupplierID", "Amount"}, Payments, {"SupplierID", "Amount"}, "Payments", JoinKind.Inner),
    #"Filtered Rows" = Table.SelectRows(#"Expanded Payments", each [DisbursementID] < [PairDisbursementID] and [PaymentDate] <> [PairPaymentDate]),
```

![What a table merged with itself holds, and which filter removes which rows.](../figures/chapter-16-audit-monitoring/IF-16-6-B.pdf)

**Check:** 113 rows, one for each pair. **Watch for:** the filters. With none, the merge returns 40,090 rows: 39,864 payments paired with themselves and every pair in both orders. Dates alone leave 226, each pair twice; the keys alone leave 113, the right count, because no two payments of one supplier and amount share a date. The exercise asks for both, so a roll does not break it.

### Requirement (2): The days apart

**Step 2. Measure the pairs.**

a. Add `DaysApart`, the absolute number of days between the two dates, and `SameInvoice`, whether the two payments carry one PurchaseInvoiceID.
b. Choose **Home > Close & Apply** and add the measures Pairs, Pairs Within 30 Days and Pairs on the Same Invoice to the display folder Ex 16.6.
c. Add three cards and a **Clustered column chart** of DaysApart and Pairs, titled "Near-duplicate pairs by days apart".

```
Pairs on the Same Invoice = CALCULATE ( [Pairs], PaymentPairs[SameInvoice] = TRUE () ) + 0
```

**Check:** 113 pairs, 13 within 30 days and 6 within 14, the shortest 3 days apart and the longest 759, and none on the same recorded invoice. **Watch for:** the card of pairs on the same invoice. With no pair it would read blank, which a reader takes for an error: the `+ 0` makes it read 0.

### Requirement (3): The approvals

**Step 3. Count each employee's documents.**

a. Add the three measures below.
b. Add a **Table** of EmployeeName, JobTitle and the three measures.

```
Entries Approved = COUNTROWS ( JournalEntry )
Orders Approved =
CALCULATE (
    COUNTROWS ( PurchaseOrder ),
    TREATAS ( VALUES ( Employee[EmployeeID] ), PurchaseOrder[ApprovedByEmployeeID] )
)
Registers Approved =
CALCULATE (
    COUNTROWS ( PayrollRegister ),
    TREATAS ( VALUES ( Employee[EmployeeID] ), PayrollRegister[ApprovedByEmployeeID] )
)
```

**Check:** Employee filters JournalEntry through the approver, so Entries Approved is a plain count. It does not filter PurchaseOrder or PayrollRegister, so TREATAS applies the employees in the filter context to their ApprovedByEmployeeID. **Watch for:** a plain count of orders. Every row then reads 18,832, all the orders, because the employee never reaches the table, and the table grows from 11 rows to 120, one for every employee.

### Requirement (4): The shares

**Step 4. Divide by each process's own documents.**

a. Add Entries Share, Orders Share and Registers Share.
b. Add the shares to the table, sorted by Orders Approved, titled "Documents approved, and their share of each process".
c. Open the Monitoring page and compare the table with the bar chart of exceptions by approver.

```
Entries Share = DIVIDE ( [Entries Approved], CALCULATE ( COUNTROWS ( JournalEntry ), REMOVEFILTERS ( Employee ) ) )
```

**Check:** Cynthia Reyes (Chief Financial Officer) approved 18,817 orders, 99.92%, and 43 entries, 5.05%. David Roth (Controller) approved 802 entries, 94.24%. Tasha Todd (Accounting Manager) approved 8,197 registers, 100%, and 6 entries. The other 15 orders are spread over eight employees, one of them the departed Account Executive with 3. The bar chart puts Tasha Todd first (89 exceptions) and Cynthia Reyes second (21). **Watch for:** the denominator of Entries Share. Dividing by the count of entries in the employee's own context reads `100%` for every approver, because it divides a number by itself; REMOVEFILTERS on Employee restores the whole population.

![A mock of the model answer's page, the near-duplicate pairs and the table of approval shares.](../figures/chapter-16-audit-monitoring/IF-16-6-A.pdf)

### Requirement (5): The note

**Step 5. Interpret.** A pair of payments on different invoices is two payments that happen to match, which two invoices of one amount could explain. It is an anomaly to look at, not a duplicate payment, until the documents show one bill paid twice, which two invoice records of the same bill would hide from this test. The shares show that each of three processes rests on one approver. That is a design question no exception shows: segregation of duties, who approves when that person is away, and who approves that person's own documents.

**Instructor check.** In **DAX query view**, run this and compare it with the Expected results.

```dax
EVALUATE ROW ( "Pairs", [Pairs], "CFO order share", ROUND ( CALCULATE ( [Orders Share], Employee[EmployeeID] = 2 ), 4 ), "Orders approved by others", CALCULATE ( [Orders Approved], Employee[EmployeeID] <> 2 ) )
```

## What a strong answer says

- **(2)** The pairs, the 30-day count and the finding that none refers to one recorded invoice, so they are near-duplicates within the test's scope, not proven duplicates (Chapter 8).
- **(4)** Three approvers each hold nearly or exactly all of a process, which the bar chart's counts hide: it ranks people by exceptions, not by reliance.
- **(5)** The anomaly reasoning, then the design question with its three parts, and a recommendation: a second approver, and a test that the approver's own documents have another approver.

A strong answer reads the shares as a design finding and says what control would change them. A weak one lists the top approvers.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **No filter on the merge.** *Symptom:* 40,090 rows. *Response:* ask what a payment is paired with.
- **Only the dates.** *Symptom:* 226 pairs. *Response:* ask whether the pair A and B differs from B and A.
- **A plain count of orders.** *Symptom:* 18,832 on every row. *Response:* ask whether Employee reaches PurchaseOrder.
- **A share of itself.** *Symptom:* `100%` for every approver. *Response:* ask which rows the denominator counts.
- **Counts read as concentration.** *Symptom:* "the Accounting Manager is the worst approver". *Response:* ask what share of the documents each approved.
- **Pairs reported as duplicates.** *Symptom:* a finding of 113 duplicate payments. *Response:* ask whether any pair pays one invoice.
