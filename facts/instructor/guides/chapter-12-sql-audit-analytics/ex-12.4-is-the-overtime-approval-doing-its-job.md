# Exercise 12.4: Is the Overtime Approval Doing Its Job?

{{ header() }}

## Purpose and learning outcomes

Tutorial 12.3 found that overtime above half an hour is almost always approved and that most of its growth sits on surge days. The controller asks whether the approvals are a control over that growth or only a record of it. The audit mindset: a control is judged by what it can prevent, not by whether it exists. An approval that never changes a request, always arrives on the work date, comes from one person and gives the same reasons in every quarter records overtime; it does not authorize it. Finding no exceptions is the finding, and the tests of design need no exception to say something. The analytics type is diagnostic.

Students can:

- describe an approval control by the mix of its reasons, its approvers, the hours it changes and its timing (Requirements 1 to 3);
- compare the overtime of surge days with other days (Requirement 4);
- tell a control that operates from one designed to prevent, and propose two changes (Requirement 5).

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution Core S2 M8 (audit evidence and tests of controls) and ISC M2; IMA Internal Control and Performance Management; IFAC IES 2 (b) and (g); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

## Before you assign

- **Files.** CharlesRiver_Work.sqlite, a new `Ex 12.4.sql`, and tests H6 to H8 of Audit.sql.
- **Data conditions.** OvertimeApproval holds ReasonCode, RequestedHours, ApprovedHours, WorkDate, ApprovedDate and the approver; TimeClockEntry links to it through OvertimeApprovalID. A surge day is a day on which every manufacturing employee who clocked in recorded the same hours (test H8). A date has no time of day, so it cannot show whether approval came before the overtime within the day.
- **Verify first.** Test O1 returns {{ g.o1_rows }} rows, {{ n_reasons|lower }} reasons for each of three years.

## Step-by-step solution

### Requirement (1): The reasons

**Step 1. Count the approvals by year and reason, with each share of the year.** The window function divides each count by the year's total.

```sql
-- Exercise 12.4, test O1: overtime approvals by year and reason code
-- Population: all 18,145 overtime approvals; expected: reasons that change with the overtime
SELECT strftime('%Y', WorkDate) AS WorkYear, ReasonCode,
    COUNT(*) AS Approvals,
    ROUND(COUNT(*) * 1.0 / SUM(COUNT(*)) OVER (
        PARTITION BY strftime('%Y', WorkDate)), 3) AS ShareOfYear
FROM OvertimeApproval
GROUP BY WorkYear, ReasonCode
ORDER BY WorkYear, ReasonCode;
```

**Check:** {{ g.o1_rows }} rows, every share close to a quarter in every year: the reasons ({{ reasons|join(', ') }}) carry no information about why overtime grew. **Watch for:** without `* 1.0` the division is an integer division and every share reads {{ g.o1_int[0]|num(1) }}.

### Requirement (2): The approvers

**Step 2. Count by approver.**

```sql
-- Exercise 12.4, test O2: approvals by approver, with the largest approver's share
-- Population: all overtime approvals; expected: approval shared among supervisors
SELECT oa.ApprovedByEmployeeID, e.JobTitle, COUNT(*) AS Approvals,
    ROUND(COUNT(*) * 1.0 / SUM(COUNT(*)) OVER (), 3) AS ShareOfApprovals
FROM OvertimeApproval AS oa
    INNER JOIN Employee AS e ON e.EmployeeID = oa.ApprovedByEmployeeID
GROUP BY oa.ApprovedByEmployeeID, e.JobTitle
ORDER BY Approvals DESC;
```

**Check:** {{ g.o2_rows }} rows. The Production Manager approved {{ manager_n|count }} of {{ n|count }} approvals, {{ manager_share|pct }}. **Watch for:** grouping by JobTitle gives the same {{ g.by_title }} rows here, because no two approvers share a title; it fails on other data, as the approvers of Tutorial 12.2 show. Group by the key.

### Requirement (3): The hours and the timing

**Step 3. Compare approved with requested hours, and approval with work date.** Each count is a SUM over a CASE.

```sql
-- Exercise 12.4, test O3: approved against requested hours, and approval against work date
-- Population: all overtime approvals; expected: some requests reduced, approvals in advance
SELECT COUNT(*) AS Approvals,
    SUM(CASE WHEN ApprovedHours = RequestedHours THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
        AS HoursAsRequested,
    SUM(CASE WHEN ApprovedHours < RequestedHours THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
        AS HoursReduced,
    SUM(CASE WHEN ApprovedDate < WorkDate THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
        AS ApprovedInAdvance,
    SUM(CASE WHEN ApprovedDate = WorkDate THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
        AS ApprovedOnWorkDate,
    SUM(CASE WHEN ApprovedDate > WorkDate THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)
        AS ApprovedAfter
FROM OvertimeApproval;
```

**Check:** one row: {{ g.o3[0]|count }} approvals, {{ g.o3[1]|count }} for exactly the hours requested, {{ g.o3[2] }} reduced, {{ g.o3[3] }} approved in advance, {{ g.o3[4]|count }} on the work date, {{ g.o3[5] }} after it. No request was cut and none was approved on an earlier day. **Watch for:** `<=` in place of `<` reads {{ g.o3_le|count }} approvals as given in advance. The test finds nothing, and that is evidence about the design.

### Requirement (4): The surge days

**Step 4. Find who approved the surge-day overtime and when.** A SurgeDays CTE is the one of tests H8 and H9: manufacturing days with `HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT tc.RegularHours + tc.OvertimeHours) = 1 AND COUNT(*) > 1`. The clock entries of those days are joined to the approval and its approver with LEFT JOINs, and a CASE names the timing.

```sql
SELECT ap.JobTitle AS ApprovedBy,
    CASE
        WHEN oa.ApprovedDate IS NULL THEN 'No approval'
        WHEN oa.ApprovedDate < tc.WorkDate THEN 'Before the work date'
        WHEN oa.ApprovedDate = tc.WorkDate THEN 'On the work date'
        ELSE 'After the work date'
    END AS ApprovalTiming,
    COUNT(DISTINCT tc.WorkDate) AS SurgeDays, COUNT(*) AS ClockEntries,
    ROUND(SUM(tc.OvertimeHours), 0) AS OvertimeHours
FROM TimeClockEntry AS tc
    INNER JOIN SurgeDays AS sd ON sd.WorkDate = tc.WorkDate
    INNER JOIN Employee AS e ON e.EmployeeID = tc.EmployeeID
    INNER JOIN CostCenter AS cc ON cc.CostCenterID = e.CostCenterID
    LEFT JOIN OvertimeApproval AS oa
        ON oa.OvertimeApprovalID = tc.OvertimeApprovalID
    LEFT JOIN Employee AS ap ON ap.EmployeeID = oa.ApprovedByEmployeeID
WHERE cc.CostCenterName = 'Manufacturing'
GROUP BY ApprovedBy, ApprovalTiming;
```

**Check:** one row: {{ g.o4[0] }}, {{ g.o4[1]|lower }}, {{ g.o4[2] }} days, {{ g.o4[3]|count }} clock entries, {{ g.o4[4]|num }} hours of overtime. **Watch for:** comparing regular hours alone, `COUNT(DISTINCT tc.RegularHours) = 1`, finds {{ g.o4_reg_days }} days, and entries with no approval appear on them.

**Step 5. Compare surge-day overtime with other days by year.** The same SurgeDays CTE, now with a LEFT JOIN.

```sql
SELECT strftime('%Y', tc.WorkDate) AS WorkYear,
    ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN sd.WorkDate IS NOT NULL
        THEN tc.OvertimeHours ELSE 0 END), 0) AS SurgeDayOvertime,
    ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN sd.WorkDate IS NULL
        THEN tc.OvertimeHours ELSE 0 END), 0) AS OtherDayOvertime,
    ROUND(SUM(tc.OvertimeHours), 0) AS Overtime
FROM TimeClockEntry AS tc
    INNER JOIN Employee AS e ON e.EmployeeID = tc.EmployeeID
    INNER JOIN CostCenter AS cc ON cc.CostCenterID = e.CostCenterID
    LEFT JOIN SurgeDays AS sd ON sd.WorkDate = tc.WorkDate
WHERE cc.CostCenterName = 'Manufacturing'
GROUP BY WorkYear
ORDER BY WorkYear;
```

**Check:** surge days {% for r in g.o5 %}{{ r[1]|num }}{{ ", " if not loop.last else "" }}{% endfor %} hours; other days {% for r in g.o5 %}{{ r[2]|num }}{{ ", " if not loop.last else "" }}{% endfor %}. Overtime on surge days grew by {{ g.growth_surge|num }} hours, and on other days by -{{ (g.growth_other|abs)|num }}: all the growth sits on days one manager scheduled and approved. **Watch for:** an INNER JOIN to SurgeDays drops the other days, and other-day overtime reads {{ g.o5_inner[0][2]|num }}.

### Requirement (5): The findings

**Step 6. Write the list.** The control looks like a record of overtime, not an authorization: reasons that say nothing, one approver for most approvals, hours never changed, approval on the work date. Two changes make it a control: approval before the shift with a weekly limit per employee and a second approver outside production above it, and reason codes tied to a work order or documented backlog and reviewed monthly by someone who does not schedule the surge days. State the limit of the evidence: the requests and their timestamps are not in the data.

## What a strong answer says

- **(1) to (3)** Four tests, one conclusion: the approval records overtime. A strong answer says what each result would have looked like in a working control (reasons that move, hours that change, approval before the shift).
- **(4)** One manager scheduled and approved all the surge-day overtime, and it is all the growth.
- **(5)** The design changes are specific (a limit, a second approver, linked reasons) and the answer names what the data cannot show. A weak answer says "the control failed", though no exception exists.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **Shares of zero.** *Symptom:* every ShareOfYear reads {{ g.o1_int[0]|num(1) }}. *Response:* ask what 1 divided by 4 is in integers.
- **`<=` for `<`.** *Symptom:* every approval "in advance". *Response:* ask what a date without a time can show.
- **Surge days from regular hours.** *Symptom:* {{ g.o4_reg_days }} days. *Response:* ask what the overtime does to the pattern.
- **An inner join to the surge days.** *Symptom:* other-day overtime of {{ g.o5_inner[0][2]|num }}. *Response:* ask which rows the join dropped.
- **"No exceptions, so no problem".** *Symptom:* a clean conclusion. *Response:* ask what the control could have stopped.
