# Exercise 15.5: Testing the Forecast Before Trusting It

{{ header() }}

## Purpose and learning outcomes

A manager has seen the forecast in the Analytics pane and wants it in the plan. Before an auditor accepts it, the auditor does what Chapter 7 did with its own forecasts: run it on a quarter whose result is known and compare it with simple benchmarks. The forecast is computed inside the visual, so no measure can return it, and the exercise is a lesson in evidence: a number that cannot be read from the model needs a test and a disclosure before anyone relies on it. The analytics type is predictive, judged by an analytical procedure.

Students can:

- prepare a continuous date axis for a forecast (Requirement 1);
- add a forecast, and read its points and intervals (Requirements 2 and 3);
- compare a forecast with the actual quarter and three benchmarks on one definition of error (Requirement 4);
- test the forecast's sensitivity to the start-up month (Requirement 5);
- write an audit note on fitness and disclosure (Requirement 6).

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution Core S2 M9 (audit procedures, analytical procedures and audit data analytics) and BAR M10 (advanced data analytics); IMA Data Analytics and Insights (applied forecasting, predictive and prescriptive analytics, statistical analysis); IFAC IES 2 (f) (audit) and IES 4 (a) (professional skepticism and professional judgment); AACSB A5 parts 5.1 and 5.2 (technology judgment, interpretation and communication).

Individual or pairs, about 75 minutes: 10 for the column, 15 for the chart and forecast, 10 to record the points, 15 to compare, 10 to repeat from February, 10 for the note, 5 spare. ### Requirement (4): Against the actual and the benchmarks

**Step 4. Compute one error for every method.** The error of a quarter is the forecast total over the actual less one, so a positive error means over-forecast, and its size, not its sign, ranks the methods. The monthly error is the average of the absolute difference between each month's forecast and actual, over the actual, across the three months. Calculate both for every method. Chapter 7's benchmarks for the same quarter, fitted from February {{ g.first }}, are in the table below. Students compute their forecast's error the same way and place it beside them.

**Watch for:** mixing the two measures of error. The quarter error nets the months' misses against each other, the monthly error does not, and the ranking changes: the same quarter last year is {{ errors.last.quarter|spct(2) }} by the quarter and {{ errors.last.monthly|pct(2) }} by the month.

### Requirement (5): Without the start-up month

**Step 5. Start the chart in February.**

a. Copy the chart, and change the months to February {{ g.first }} through September {{ g.y }}; the model answer titles it "{{ g.t_feb }}". Treat the January run as the run that includes the start-up month, and compare the February run with Chapter 7's benchmarks, which were fitted on the same months.
b. Repeat Step 3 and report how much each month and the quarter changed.

**Check:** the change is read from Desktop. The cause is checkable: January {{ g.first }} is {{ start|pct(0) }} of the other months of its year, and the Checks tab proves the ratio.

### Requirement (6): The audit note

**Step 6. Outline the note.** A note may recommend adopting the forecast, a limited trial or rejecting it. Whichever it recommends, it names an error and the planning risk, and it asks that the forecast travel with its test, because required disclosures are necessary but not sufficient and more forecast origins should be tested before the method is adopted. The disclosures are the fitted period and the treatment of the start-up month, the hindcast error beside the benchmark it beat or did not beat, and the limit of {{ years }} years of history for any seasonal pattern. In the model answer the mean benchmark has the smallest quarter error of the methods compared, while the two recorded forecasts have a smaller average error by month than the mean. Ask the student which error the plan needs, and whether the forecast beat the benchmarks on it.

## What a strong answer says

- **(4)** The forecast's quarter error beside the three benchmarks, on one definition, then the same for the monthly error, and a sentence on which method did best on each.
- **(5)** How much the forecast moved when one month went, and why: a start-up month at {{ start|pct(0) }} of its neighbors pulls a method that weights history.
- **(6)** The forecast is not fit unless the test, the fitted period, the start-up treatment and the benchmark come with it; {{ years }} years give limited evidence of a stable seasonal pattern.

A strong answer recommends adoption, a limited trial or rejection using a named error and the planning risk, and a reasoned rejection supported by the comparisons earns full credit. A weak one trusts the line because it looks smooth.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **Benchmarks refitted on the start-up month.** *Symptom:* a mean error of {{ g.with_start.mean|spct(2) }} and a trend of {{ g.with_start.trend|spct(2) }}, against {{ errors.mean.quarter|spct(2) }} and {{ errors.trend.quarter|spct(2) }}. *Response:* ask which months Chapter 7 fitted.
- **No hindcast.** *Symptom:* the forecast runs into the next year. *Response:* ask which quarter the student already knows.
- **A text axis.** *Symptom:* no forecast to add. *Response:* ask which column is a date.
- **Intervals added.** *Symptom:* an interval for the quarter built from three. *Response:* ask whether three intervals are three independent errors.
- **Two error measures mixed.** *Symptom:* the forecast's quarter error against the benchmarks' monthly errors. *Response:* ask what each error nets.
- **A smooth line trusted.** *Symptom:* no disclosure in the note. *Response:* ask what a manager needs to know to weigh the number.
