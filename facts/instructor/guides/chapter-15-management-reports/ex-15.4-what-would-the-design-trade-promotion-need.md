# Exercise 15.4: What Would the Design Trade Promotion Need?

{{ header() }}

## Purpose and learning outcomes

The sales manager asks how much more volume the Design Trade Customer Promotion would need, at each discount, to pay for itself. The Part II case answered once with Goal Seek. The exercise turns that answer into a page the manager can use: a slider for the discount and a measure that returns the break-even lift. The lift is a condition, not a forecast. What decides the advice is the evidence of the case, which found no unusual increase in volume in any promotion's months. The analytics type is prescriptive, resting on a model of unit margins.

Students can:

- create a what-if parameter with a whole-number range and say why (Requirement 1);
- scope measures to one promotion and one year (Requirement 2);
- write the break-even lift from unit margins (Requirement 3);
- record the lift at five discounts and compare one with the Part II case (Requirement 4);
- weigh a required lift against evidence and advise (Requirement 5).

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution BAR M8 (planning techniques, forecasting and decision models) and BAR M10 (advanced data analytics); IMA Strategy, Planning, and Performance (decision analysis) and Data Analytics and Insights (applied forecasting, predictive and prescriptive analytics); IFAC IES 2 (b) (management accounting) and IES 2 (i) (using information and communications technologies to analyze data); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

a. Choose **Modeling > New parameter > Numeric range**.
b. Set **Name** to `Discount`, **Data type** to **Whole number**, **Minimum** to 0, **Maximum** to 20, **Increment** to 1 and the default to 12.
c. Keep **Add slicer to this page**.

```dax jinja

```

**Check:** the table Discount has {{ steps }} rows, from 0 to 20, and Discount Value returns the slider's value, 12 until the slider moves. The slider reads whole percents, so every measure divides its value by 100. The Checks tab proves the row count and the two ends.

**Watch for:** a decimal range from 0 to 0.2 in steps of 0.01. In Power BI Desktop {{ g.version }} it stopped at 0.19, {{ g.n_decimal }} values, so the manager could not choose `0.20`. A step of 0.01 is not stored exactly, and twenty of them add to slightly more than 0.2, which is the likely reason. The whole-number range avoids the question, and the check is that its slider reaches 0 and 20.

### Requirement (2): The unit measures

**Step 2. Add the three measures.**

a. Select **Key Measures** and choose **Modeling > New measure** for each.
b. Type `Ex 15.4` in **Display folder** in **Model view**.

```dax jinja
{{ g.m_units }}
{{ g.m_price }}
{{ g.m_cost }}
```

**Check:** {{ units|num(2) }} units, ${{ price|money }} of price before discount a unit and ${{ cost|money }} of standard cost a unit, the Part II case's inputs. The filter names the promotion and the year.

**Watch for:** a price taken from revenue divided by units. That is the price after the discount, ${{ g.net_price|money }}, and the lift then counts the discount twice: {{ g.net_lift|pct(1) }} at {{ (g.disc * 100)|num(0) }} percent in place of {{ lifts[3][1]|pct(1) }}.

### Requirement (3): The break-even lift

**Step 3. Write the measure.**

```dax jinja
{{ g.m_lift }}
```

**Check:** the contribution a unit earns without the discount is ${{ g.contrib_without|money }} and with a {{ (g.disc * 100)|num(0) }} percent discount ${{ g.contrib_with|money }}; their ratio less one is {{ lifts[3][1]|pct(1) }}, the break-even lift, so volume must rise by that share for discounted sales to earn what full-price sales earned.

**What the model assumes.** The scenario holds the observed product and customer mix, the price before discount, the standard cost per unit and the commission rate fixed, and assumes the plant has capacity for the extra volume. Standard cost is the case's cost proxy and includes allocated fixed overhead, so the lift is a standard-cost margin and not a separately verified incremental-cost estimate. The model leaves out setup costs and cannibalization, so the lift is a condition for preserving the modeled contribution, not a forecast of demand. The comparison with the promotion months stays observational. Students who state these limits earn a plus, and the assigned range of discounts creates no case of a nonpositive contribution.

**Watch for:** two silent failures. Leaving the division by 100 makes the discount 12, the denominator turns negative and the lift reads {{ g.wrong_scale|pct(1) }}. Leaving the commission out reads {{ g.no_com_12|pct(1) }} at {{ (g.disc * 100)|num(0) }} percent and {{ g.no_com_5|pct(1) }} at 5 percent: about a point low at 12 percent, and plausible enough to pass unless the student compares with the case.

### Requirement (4): The page and the record

**Step 4. Build the page and read five discounts.**

a. Add a page named `Ex 15.4` with the slider, cards for the five measures, and a **Table** with Discount and Break-even Lift.
b. Select the slider, choose **Format > Edit interactions**, and click the **None** icon above the table.
c. Read the lift at 5, 8, 10, 12 and 15 percent.

**Check:** the table, titled "{{ g.t_table }}", lists every discount and the cards follow the slider. The lift at {{ (g.disc * 100)|num(0) }} percent equals the Part II case.

**Watch for:** a table with one row. Without step (b) the slicer filters the table to the chosen discount. A slicer set to several selections makes SELECTEDVALUE return its default, so the cards stay at 12 whatever the student picks.

### Requirement (5): The advice

**Step 5. Weigh the lift against the evidence.** The Part II case measured volume in each promotion's months against the other months of its year and found no lift outside ordinary variation: from {{ low|pct(1) }} to {{ high|pct(1) }}. A {{ (g.disc * 100)|num(0) }} percent discount needs {{ lifts[3][1]|pct(1) }}. Even 5 percent needs {{ lifts[0][1]|pct(1) }}, {{ five }} the largest increase ever measured. The advice is not to repeat the promotion at 12 percent, and, if the manager wants to test one, to offer a small discount to a defined group of customers and compare its volume with a comparable group.

## What a strong answer says

- **(1)** The integer range avoids endpoint surprises, and the student verifies the values and both endpoints. The decimal range stopping at 0.19 is the author's observed result in Power BI Desktop {{ g.version }}, not a result every build must give.
- **(4)** The five lifts, and that the 12 percent figure equals the case.
- **(5)** The required lift against the measured range; the lift is a break-even condition, not a forecast; the volume of the promotion months cannot show what the promotion caused, so a test with a comparison group is the next step.

A strong answer ends with an action and its limit. A weak one repeats the table.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **A decimal range.** *Symptom:* the largest choice is {{ g.last_decimal }}. *Response:* check the generated values and the supported build, then use the integer range and divide by 100.
- **No division by 100.** *Symptom:* a lift of {{ g.wrong_scale|pct(1) }}. *Response:* ask what the slider's 12 means in the formula.
- **Net price as the price.** *Symptom:* {{ g.net_lift|pct(1) }} at 12 percent. *Response:* ask where the discount is taken in the formula.
- **Commission left out.** *Symptom:* {{ g.no_com_12|pct(1) }} at 12 percent. *Response:* ask what a sale costs beyond its standard cost.
- **A one-row table.** *Symptom:* only the chosen discount. *Response:* ask what the slider filters.
- **The lift as a forecast.** *Symptom:* "volume will rise by {{ lifts[3][1]|pct(1) }}". *Response:* ask what the evidence says volume did.
