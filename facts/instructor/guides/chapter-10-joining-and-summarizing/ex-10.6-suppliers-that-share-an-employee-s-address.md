# Exercise 10.6: Suppliers That Share an Employee's Address

{{ header() }}

## Purpose and learning outcomes

In Exercise 5.5 students matched supplier addresses against employee addresses in Excel. The audit senior wants the test repeated with a join, the payments to the matching suppliers measured, and a test added for customers that share a name. The exercise is the chapter's fan-out lesson in an audit setting: invoices and payments hang from the same supplier, and a join of one to the other multiplies them. The analytics type is diagnostic, and the memo is a judgment about evidence.

By the end, students can:

- join two master tables on an attribute and read the matches (outcome 1);
- total two child tables for the same parent without a fan-out, with derived tables or separate queries (outcomes 4 and 5);
- find duplicated names with GROUP BY and HAVING (outcome 3);
- say what an exact join proves and what it cannot, and recommend next procedures.

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution Core S2 M5 (risk of fraud and noncompliance) and BAR M10 (data models and joins); IMA Reporting and Control (internal control) and Data Analytics and Insights (data analysis); IFAC IES 2 (f) and IES 4 (a) (professional skepticism and judgment); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

## Step-by-step solution

### Requirement (1): The matches

**Step 1. Join Supplier to Employee on Address.**

```sql
-- Requirement (1): suppliers whose address is an employee's
-- Population: Supplier and Employee; expected: six matches, all approved
SELECT s.SupplierID, s.SupplierName, s.IsApproved,
    e.EmployeeID, e.EmployeeName, e.JobTitle, s.Address
FROM Supplier AS s
    INNER JOIN Employee AS e ON e.Address = s.Address
ORDER BY s.SupplierID;
```

**Step 2. Check that no address belongs to two employees.** A hint: the model script groups Employee by Address with `HAVING COUNT(*) > 1`.

**Check:** {{ n_word }} rows, all approved: {% for m in matches %}supplier {{ m.supplier }} {{ m.name }} with employee {{ m.employee }} ({{ m.title }}){{ "; " if not loop.last else "" }}{% endfor %}. The Step 2 check returns no rows, so each supplier meets one employee. **Watch for:** a join that ignores capitals and surrounding spaces finds the same {{ g.loose }} matches, so on this data the exact join misses nothing of that kind. Addresses written differently, an abbreviation or a suite number, would still be missed.

### Requirement (2): Invoices and payments

**Step 3. Total the invoices and the payments of each matching supplier apart, and join the totals.**

```sql
-- Requirement (2): invoices and payments of the matching suppliers through 2026-12-31
-- Population: PurchaseInvoice and DisbursementPayment dated through 2026-12-31; expected: 6 suppliers, each table totaled on its own
SELECT s.SupplierID, s.SupplierName,
    inv.Invoices, inv.Invoiced, pay.Payments, pay.Paid
FROM Supplier AS s
    INNER JOIN Employee AS e ON e.Address = s.Address
    INNER JOIN (
        SELECT SupplierID, COUNT(*) AS Invoices,
            ROUND(SUM(GrandTotal), 2) AS Invoiced
        FROM PurchaseInvoice
        WHERE InvoiceDate <= '2026-12-31'
        GROUP BY SupplierID
    ) AS inv ON inv.SupplierID = s.SupplierID
    INNER JOIN (
        SELECT SupplierID, COUNT(*) AS Payments,
            ROUND(SUM(Amount), 2) AS Paid
        FROM DisbursementPayment
        WHERE PaymentDate <= '2026-12-31'
        GROUP BY SupplierID
    ) AS pay ON pay.SupplierID = s.SupplierID
ORDER BY s.SupplierID;
```

**Step 4. Run the single-query join, for contrast.**

```sql
-- Requirement (2): the fan-out of joining invoices to payments, supplier 1
-- Population: supplier 1's invoices and payments through 2026-12-31; expected: 152,880 rows, not 390 or 392
SELECT COUNT(*) AS JoinedRows
FROM PurchaseInvoice AS pi
    INNER JOIN DisbursementPayment AS dp
        ON dp.SupplierID = pi.SupplierID
WHERE pi.SupplierID = 1 AND pi.InvoiceDate <= '2026-12-31'
    AND dp.PaymentDate <= '2026-12-31';
```

### Requirement (3): Shared customer names

**Step 6. Find the names shared by more than one customer.**

```sql
-- Requirement (3): customer names shared by more than one CustomerID
-- Population: Customer; expected: Brown LLC and Smith Ltd
SELECT CustomerName, COUNT(*) AS Customers
FROM Customer
GROUP BY CustomerName
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1
ORDER BY CustomerName;
```

**Step 7. List those customers with their segments.**

```sql
-- Requirement (3): those customers with their segments
-- Population: Customer, shared names; expected: 4 customers
SELECT CustomerID, CustomerName, CustomerSegment, City, Region,
    IsActive
FROM Customer
WHERE CustomerName IN (
    SELECT CustomerName
    FROM Customer
    GROUP BY CustomerName
    HAVING COUNT(*) > 1
)
ORDER BY CustomerName, CustomerID;
```

## What a strong answer says

- **Requirement (4).** The join compares every supplier address with every employee address, exactly and again next year, so it cannot overlook a match that the eye would miss in two long lists. It cannot find addresses written differently, and a match does not prove a relationship: a shared address may be a coincidence, an office building or a family member, and only evidence outside the database can tell.
- **Requirement (5).** The memo lists the {{ n_word }} approved suppliers with the employee and job title of each, the invoices and payments totaled separately through {{ end }}, the fan-out as the reason, and the shared customer names. It recommends the vendor setup records (who requested and approved each supplier, on what evidence), the employees' conflict-of-interest declarations, whether the matching employees can request, approve or receive purchases from these suppliers, a sample of the suppliers' invoices vouched to receipts, and a check whether the shared customer names are different customers or duplicates.
- **A plus.** The open invoices of supplier {{ big.id }} (Step 5), with the note that they include the two invoices moved to notes payable, so they are not the payables balance. The exercise does not ask for them, so they earn no core credit.

A weak memo calls the matches fraud, quotes totals taken from a fanned-out join, or recommends no procedure that could separate a coincidence from a relationship.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **Invoices joined to payments on the supplier.** *Symptom:* {{ fan|count }} rows and a GrandTotal of ${{ g.one_total|money }}. *Response:* ask how many rows the two tables hold apart.
- **Joined on the invoice key.** *Symptom:* {{ g.by_invoice_n|count }} rows and a total of ${{ g.by_invoice_total|money }}. *Response:* ask what a partly paid invoice does to the join.
- **No payment-date condition.** *Symptom:* {{ g.late_n|count }} payments for supplier {{ first }}. *Response:* ask which payments belong to the period.
- **A grouping that hides a name.** *Symptom:* {{ g.seg_name }} only. *Response:* ask which columns decide whether two customers are the same.
- **A match called proof.** *Symptom:* a memo that names a conflict of interest. *Response:* ask what else could explain a shared address.
- **No next procedures.** *Symptom:* findings only. *Response:* ask what evidence would confirm or clear each match.
