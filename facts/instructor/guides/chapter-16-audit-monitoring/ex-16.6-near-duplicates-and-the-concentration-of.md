# Exercise 16.6: Near-Duplicates and the Concentration of Approval

{{ header() }}

## Purpose and learning outcomes

The chief audit executive asks for two additions: the near-duplicate payments of Chapter 8, a second anomaly test beside Benford's Law, and a measure of how much of each process rests on one approver, which the exceptions by approver do not show. The technical lessons are a table merged with itself and TREATAS. The analytics type is diagnostic, and the second part is a design question: the finding is not a rule that fails but a control with one person in it.

Students can:

- merge a table with itself and keep each pair once (Requirement 1);
- measure the days between two payments and test whether a pair pays one invoice (Requirement 2);
- count the documents an employee approved in tables the employee is not related to (Requirement 3);
- compute shares with the right denominator and compare them with the exceptions by approver (Requirement 4);
- explain why a near-duplicate is an anomaly, and what concentration says about the design of the approvals (Requirement 5).

**Competencies practiced.** CPA Evolution Core S2 M5 (risk of fraud and noncompliance) and ISC M2 (tests of internal controls); IMA Reporting and Control (internal control); IFAC IES 2 (f) (audit) and (g) (governance, risk management and internal control); AACSB A5 part 5.1.

### Requirement (5): The note

**Step 5. Interpret.** A pair of payments on different invoices is two payments that happen to match, which two invoices of one amount explain. It is an anomaly to look at, not a duplicate payment, until an invoice is found paid twice. The shares show that each of three processes rests on one approver. That is a design question no exception shows: segregation of duties, who approves when that person is away, and who approves that person's own documents.

## What a strong answer says

- **(2)** The pairs, the 30-day count and the finding that none pays one invoice: near-duplicates, not duplicates (Chapter 8).
- **(4)** Three approvers each hold nearly or exactly all of a process, which the bar chart's counts hide: it ranks people by exceptions, not by reliance.
- **(5)** The anomaly reasoning, then the design question with its three parts, and a recommendation: a second approver, and a test that the approver's own documents have another approver.

A strong answer reads the shares as a design finding and says what control would change them. A weak one lists the top approvers.

## Common mistakes and how to respond

- **No filter on the merge.** *Symptom:* {{ x.merge_rows|count }} rows. *Response:* ask what a payment is paired with.
- **Only the dates.** *Symptom:* {{ x.date_only }} pairs. *Response:* ask whether the pair A and B differs from B and A.
- **A plain count of orders.** *Symptom:* {{ x.total_orders|count }} on every row. *Response:* ask whether Employee reaches PurchaseOrder.
- **A share of itself.** *Symptom:* `100%` for every approver. *Response:* ask which rows the denominator counts.
- **Counts read as concentration.** *Symptom:* "the Accounting Manager is the worst approver". *Response:* ask what share of the documents each approved.
- **Pairs reported as duplicates.** *Symptom:* a finding of {{ pairs }} duplicate payments. *Response:* ask whether any pair pays one invoice.

## Extensions and discussion questions

- Add the pairs within 30 days to the register as an anomaly row, kept apart from the rule tests.
- Measure the shares by year and say whether the concentration is new.
- Discuss: three processes each rest on one approver. Which would you change first, and why?
- Discuss: the exceptions by approver put one person first. What would the report say if the approvals were spread over three people?
